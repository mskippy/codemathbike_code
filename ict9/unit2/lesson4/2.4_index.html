2.4 Assembly: Going Live on GitHub

You've built the structure (HTML), the style (CSS), and the interactivity (JavaScript). Right now, though, your site only exists on your H: drive, so nobody else can see it. Today the Web Department ships. You'll create a repository on GitHub, use GitHub Desktop to connect it to your computer, and move your site into it. Then you'll get an AI to clean up your code, push it all to GitHub, and turn it into a real, live website with its own URL. Work through the tasks in order, because each one depends on the one before it.

Before You Start

  1. Open your project folder in VS Code, the same H:\ICT 9\2_web_development\your-slug folder you've used all unit. No new download needed.
  2. Check the Explorer sidebar. You should see index.html, about.html, work.html, and your css, js, and images folders directly inside it, with no extra folder in between.
  3. Have a browser tab open and ready. You'll be creating an account and signing in during class.
  4. Find GitHub Desktop in the Start menu. You'll use it from Task 3 on.

Four words you'll hear today

  • Repository (repo): a project folder that Git is keeping track of. Yours lives on GitHub and on your H: drive.
  • Commit: a save point. It's a snapshot of every file at one moment, with a short message describing what changed. You can always go back to one.
  • Push: sending your commits from your computer up to GitHub (the Push origin button in GitHub Desktop).
  • GitHub Pages: a free GitHub feature that turns a repo into a live website.

Task 1 — Pre-flight check: polish and privacy

Once your site is live, anyone on the internet can see it. Your teacher, your family, a future employer, and total strangers can all open it. Before anything gets published, go through this checklist.

Polish

  1. Open all three pages in Live Preview and click every nav link. Every page should load, with no broken links.
  2. Check every file name. Names should be all lowercase, with no spaces. avatar.png is fine, but My Avatar.PNG isn't. Rename any that break the rule, then update the src or href that points to them.
  3. Your home page must be called exactly index.html and sit directly in your project folder, not inside a subfolder.
Common mistake: Windows doesn't care about capital letters, but the web server does. If your HTML says images/avatar.png and the file is really Avatar.png, the image works on your computer and is broken on your live site. Fix it now, while it's easy.

Privacy

Go through all three pages, including your 20 Questions, and remove anything on this list:

  • Your last name (your first name, or first name plus last initial, is fine)
  • Your school name, home address, neighbourhood, or phone number
  • Your birthday or exact age
  • Photos of your face or of other people. Your cartoon avatar is exactly what it's for.
  • Personal email or social media handles

Quick test: would you be fine with this page being printed and taped up in the hallway? If not, take it out.

Oversharing audit: your 20 Questions

The list above catches the obvious stuff. Oversharing is sneakier. Each answer on its own seems harmless, but put together, they can add up to a lot: where you'll be on Saturday, what your passwords might be, or enough clues for a stranger to work out who you are. Time to look at your 20 Questions the way a stranger would.

  1. Open about.html and read your 20 answers slowly, as if you were a stranger who has never met you.
  2. Rate each answer:
    • 🟢 Safe: general likes and opinions. "Pizza." "Flight." "Night owl."
    • 🟡 Think twice: specific details that narrow down who or where you are. Team names, specific places, your pet's name.
    • 🔴 Overshare: tells a stranger where to find you, or could help someone get into your accounts. Your full birthday, your schedule, your school.
  3. Check your answers against the three overshare traps below.
  4. Rewrite every 🔴 answer and most 🟡 ones. Rewrite them rather than deleting them, so the page is still about you, just less specific (see the examples).

The three overshare traps

  • Password-reset answers. "What was your first pet's name?" and "What month were you born?" are classic security questions for resetting passwords. Questions 2 (birth month) and 11 (pets) are the ones to check. Keep your star sign or say "a dog," but leave out the month and the pet's name.
  • Location + schedule. "I skate at [park name] every Saturday morning" tells a stranger exactly where you'll be and when. Check questions 4, 6, and 18 (hobby, sports, weekends).
  • The puzzle effect. First name + a team name + a neighbourhood + a birth month might be all it takes to find you. Read all 20 answers together and ask: what could someone piece together?
Overshare 🔴🟡Still you, less specific 🟢
I'm a Leo, born August 9th.I'm a Leo, a summer baby.
My dog Biscuit is a golden retriever.I have a golden retriever who steals socks.
I play centre-back for the [club name] U15 team, Tuesdays at [field name].I play soccer, and I'm a defender.
Every Saturday I'm at [skate park] from 9 to noon.Weekends are for skateboarding.
I'd love to visit Japan because my grandparents live in Osaka.I'd love to visit Japan for the food and the arcades.
In class? Try the stranger test with a partner. Swap seats and read each other's About Me page for two minutes. Write down everything you could figure out about the person: where they might live, where they'll be and when, and anything that sounds like a password-reset answer. Swap back and compare lists. Anything on your partner's list that surprised you gets rewritten.
Be ready to explain. In your submission, name one answer you changed and why it was an overshare. If you changed nothing, explain why every answer is 🟢.
🟢 Green: all nav links work, every file name is lowercase with no spaces, nothing on any page could identify where you live or go to school, and every one of your 20 answers is 🟢 (or a 🟡 you've thought about and decided to keep).
🔴 Red: a link or image breaks after renaming a file. Check that the name in your HTML matches the new file name exactly.

Task 2 — Create your GitHub account

GitHub is where more than 100 million developers store their code, and it'll host your site for free. Your username becomes your website address, so choose it carefully.

Choose your username first

  • Your site will live at https://username.github.io. Say it out loud: would you be happy putting it on a résumé?
  • Use lowercase letters, numbers, and hyphens only.
  • No last name, no birth year, no school name. Try your slug plus a studio word, like emma-r-studio or emmar-dev.
  • Your first choice will probably be taken, since usernames are shared with every GitHub user in the world. Have two or three backups ready.

Sign up

  1. Go to github.com/signup.
  2. Enter your school email address.
  3. Create a new password you haven't used anywhere else, and write it down somewhere safe (not on a sticky note on the monitor).
  4. Enter your username and finish the puzzle check.
  5. GitHub emails you a code. Open your school email, copy the code, and paste it into GitHub.
  6. If GitHub asks you a few "personalize your experience" questions, you can skip them.
  7. Write your exact username on the class sign-in sheet your teacher shares.
Common mistake: don't get fancy with capitals or look-alike characters. GitHub URLs are always shown in lowercase, and if you can't spell your own username from memory, you'll lose your own website. Keep it simple.
🟢 Green: you're signed in at github.com, your email is verified, and your username is on the class sheet.
🔴 Red: the verification email never arrives. Check your Junk folder first, then ask your teacher.
Green

Task 3 — Create your repo and move your site in

You'll create an empty repo on GitHub, use GitHub Desktop to make a copy of it on your H: drive (that's called cloning), and then copy your site files into it.

The most important rule today: your repo name. It must be exactly your GitHub username followed by .github.io. Nothing else works.
  • Username emma-r-studio → repo name emma-r-studio.github.io ✅
  • emma-r (your slug) ❌   emma-r-studio (missing .github.io) ❌   emmar-studio.github.io (doesn't match the username) ❌
Your username is in the top-left of any of your GitHub pages, and in the class sign-in sheet. Copy it from there instead of typing it from memory.

Step 1: Make your email private

  1. On github.com, click your profile picture (top right) → Settings → Emails (left menu).
  2. Turn on Keep my email addresses private. GitHub shows you a private address that looks like 12345678+username@users.noreply.github.com. You'll pick it in GitHub Desktop in Step 3.

Step 2: Create your repo on github.com

  1. Click the + at the top right of github.com → New repository.
  2. Repository name: your username followed by .github.io (see the rule above). GitHub shows a green check when the name is available.
  3. Choose Public. (Free GitHub sites have to be public.)
  4. Leave everything else alone. Don't tick "Add a README." Click Create repository.

Step 3: Clone it to your H: drive with GitHub Desktop

  1. Open GitHub Desktop. If it asks you to sign in, choose Sign in to GitHub.com, sign in in the browser, and click Authorize.
  2. In GitHub Desktop, go to File → Options → Git. Set Email to your private …@users.noreply.github.com address from Step 1, then click Save.
  3. Back on your new, empty repo page on github.com, find the Quick setup box and click Set up in Desktop. If the browser asks to open GitHub Desktop, click Open.
  4. GitHub Desktop shows a Clone a repository box. Next to Local path, click Choose… and pick your H:\ICT 9\2_web_development folder.
  5. Check that the Local path now reads H:\ICT 9\2_web_development\your-username.github.io, then click Clone.

You now have a new, empty folder called your-username.github.io on your H: drive, sitting right next to your old your-slug folder. It's connected to GitHub.

Step 4: Copy your site into the new folder

  1. In File Explorer, open your old folder: H:\ICT 9\2_web_development\your-slug.
  2. Press Ctrl + A to select everything inside it (index.html, about.html, work.html, css, js, images), then Ctrl + C to copy.
  3. Open the new folder: H:\ICT 9\2_web_development\your-username.github.io, and press Ctrl + V to paste.
Common mistake: copy the stuff inside your slug folder, not the slug folder itself. The new folder should have index.html right inside it, not a your-slug folder with everything tucked inside. If you see one, open it, cut everything out, and paste it one level up.

Step 5: Commit and push

  1. Switch to GitHub Desktop. Your files now appear in the Changes list on the left, all ticked.
  2. In the Summary box at the bottom left, type First version of my studio code.
  3. Click Commit to main.
  4. Click Publish branch at the top. (After the first time, this button says Push origin.)

Step 6: Work from the new folder from now on

  1. In VS Code, go to File → Open Folder and open H:\ICT 9\2_web_development\your-username.github.io. (Or, in GitHub Desktop: Repository → Open in Visual Studio Code.)
  2. From today on, this is your site. Your old your-slug folder is just a backup. Don't edit it any more, or you'll get confused about which copy is real.
🟢 Green: on github.com, your repo is named your-username.github.io, says Public, and lists index.html, about.html, work.html, css, images, and js at the top level.
🔴 Red: the files aren't on github.com? You probably committed but didn't push. Click Publish branch / Push origin in GitHub Desktop. Repo has the wrong name, or the files ended up in the wrong place? See Start over cleanly below.
Start over cleanly (wrong repo name, or files in the wrong place)

It's usually faster to start fresh than to fix a broken repo. Your H: drive files are safe the whole time.

  1. On github.com: open the wrong repo → Settings → scroll to Danger Zone → Delete this repository, and follow the prompts.
  2. In GitHub Desktop: select that repo (top-left Current repository menu) → Repository → Remove…. This only takes it off GitHub Desktop's list; it doesn't delete your files.
  3. On your H: drive: if the bad clone left a folder in 2_web_development (anything other than your your-slug folder), rename it to old-attempt so it's out of the way.
  4. Redo Task 3 from Step 2, copying your files from your your-slug folder (or from whichever folder has your newest work).
Green

Task 4 — AI code review: clean up the scary code

Four weeks of copying, customizing, deleting, and re-trying leaves a mess: unclosed tags, wonky indentation, CSS rules that don't do anything, and leftover experiments. Real studios run code reviews before anything ships. Yours will be done by an AI, and you're the one who approves it.

Because your first commit is a save point, this is completely safe. If the AI breaks something, you can undo it with one click in GitHub Desktop.

Step 1: Get the AI to clean up one file

  1. Open the AI tool of your choice (any of the ones allowed in class).
  2. Copy the prompt below and paste it into the AI. Fill in the file name at the end.
  3. In VS Code (in your your-username.github.io folder), open index.html, press Ctrl + A then Ctrl + C to copy the whole file, and paste it into the AI right after the prompt.
I'm a Grade 9 student and this is my first website, built with HTML, CSS and JavaScript. Please clean up the code below.

Rules:
- Keep how the page LOOKS and WORKS exactly the same.
- Fix indentation, close any unclosed tags, fix errors, and remove anything that is duplicated or unused.
- Do NOT change any id or class names, file names, or the text on my page.
- Do NOT add new features, frameworks, libraries, or links to other websites.
- Keep it at a beginner level so I can still understand every line.
- Give me the full cleaned-up file first, then a short list of every change you made and why.

My file is called: ________

Step 2: Paste it back in and test it

  1. Copy the AI's cleaned-up file. In VS Code, select everything in index.html (Ctrl + A) and paste over it.
  2. Check Live Preview. The page should look and work exactly like it did before. Click everything, including your JavaScript buttons.

Step 3: Review every change (this is the important part)

  1. Switch to GitHub Desktop and click index.html in the Changes list. It shows every change: red is what the AI removed, and green is what it added.
  2. Read every change. For each one, you should be able to say what it does.
  3. Don't understand a change? Ask the AI: "Explain this change like I'm new to coding." Still not sure? Don't keep it.
  4. If the AI broke something, right-click the file in GitHub Desktop's Changes list → Discard changes…. You're back to your save point, and you can try again.

Step 4: Repeat, then commit

  1. Do the same for about.html, work.html, css/style.css, and js/script.js, one file at a time.
  2. When all five files are cleaned up and tested, go to GitHub Desktop, type AI code cleanup in the Summary box, and click Commit to main.
  3. Click Push origin.
Watch the AI closely. It makes mistakes too. Red flags to look for in the diff:
  • A new <script src="https://..."> or <link href="https://..."> pointing to another website that you didn't add. Delete it.
  • A changed id or class name. This silently breaks your JavaScript or CSS.
  • Your own words rewritten, or whole sections gone missing.
  • A file that suddenly got much longer. The AI probably added features you didn't ask for.
This is your AI reflection for this lesson. When you submit, answer the usual questions about this task: what you asked, what it gave you, what you kept or rejected (and why), and one thing you learned from its list of changes.
🟢 Green: your site looks and works exactly the same, the "AI code cleanup" commit shows in GitHub Desktop's History tab and has been pushed, and you can explain at least three changes the AI made.
🔴 Red: something looks or behaves differently after pasting. Discard Changes on that file and try again. You can also paste only the part of the file that's messy.
Green

Task 5 — Turn on GitHub Pages and go live

This is the moment your site hits the internet.

Step 1: Turn on GitHub Pages

  1. In GitHub Desktop, click Repository → View on GitHub (or go to your repo on github.com).
  2. Click Settings (the gear tab along the top) → Pages in the left menu.
  3. Under Build and deployment → Source, choose Deploy from a branch.
  4. Under Branch, choose main and / (root), then click Save.
  5. Wait 1 to 5 minutes. On your repo's front page, watch Deployments on the right (or the Actions tab): a yellow dot turns into a green check when it's done. Refresh the Pages settings and your live link appears at the top.

Step 2: Test it for real

  1. Open https://your-username.github.io. That's your site, live on the internet.
  2. Click every nav link and every button. Check that your avatar shows up.
  3. Open it on your phone too. It should work anywhere.
  4. Post your live URL to the 2.4 Teams assignment.
"Your site is live," but you get a 404? GitHub can't find your home page. Check your repo's file list on github.com:
  • It must be index.html, all lowercase. Index.html with a capital I doesn't count.
  • It must be at the top level of the repo, not inside a folder.
To fix a capital letter (this works for any file, like Avatar.PNG too): on github.com, click the file → the pencil icon (Edit) → change the name at the top to lowercase → Commit changes… → Commit changes. Then in GitHub Desktop, click Fetch origin → Pull origin, and rename the file to lowercase in File Explorer too. (Do it on github.com, because GitHub Desktop on Windows often doesn't notice a change that's only capital letters.)
🟢 Green: your site loads at https://your-username.github.io on a different device, every page and image works, and the URL is posted in Teams.
🔴 Red: Settings → Pages shows an upgrade message, or there's no Pages option? Your repo is private. Go to Settings → scroll to Danger Zone → Change visibility → Public, then redo Step 1. A broken image that works on your computer is almost always a capital letter in the file name (see the box above).
Green

Task 6 — Ship an update

Your site isn't finished. It'll get new projects every unit for the rest of the course. Here's the loop you'll use every time: edit in VS Code → commit in GitHub Desktop → push.

  1. In VS Code (your your-username.github.io folder), make one small change. For example, add <p>Last updated: October 2026</p> to your footer on all three pages. Save, and check Live Preview.
  2. In GitHub Desktop, your changed files show in the Changes list. Type a Summary that describes the change (Add last-updated date to footer), then click Commit to main.
  3. Click Push origin.
  4. Wait about a minute, then reload your live site with Ctrl + F5 (a hard refresh).
Good commit messages say what changed: "Add dark mode toggle" or "Fix broken avatar link." Messages like "stuff," "asdf," or "final FINAL v2" don't count. Your commit history is part of your portfolio now, and people can read it.
🟢 Green: your change shows up on the live site, and GitHub shows at least three commits with clear messages.
🔴 Red: the live site hasn't changed. Check that you clicked Push origin (not just Commit), and that you edited the files in your your-username.github.io folder, not the old slug folder. Wait another minute, and hard refresh with Ctrl + F5.
Blue

Pick one or more

Custom 404 page

Create a 404.html at the top of your repo. GitHub Pages shows it automatically whenever someone visits a page on your site that doesn't exist. Make it on-brand and funny, with a link back home.

Link preview card

Add Open Graph <meta> tags (og:title, og:description, og:image) to your <head> so your site shows a proper preview card when someone pastes the link in a chat.

Time travel

Automatic "last updated" date
